  Achromatic dream phase hoax

  The claim that children under approximately three years of age dream exclusively in achromatic (black-and-white) imagery — a phenomenon termed the "achromatic dream phase" — and that a 2019 longitudinal study led by developmental neuroscientist Céline Desrochers at the University of Montreal, published in Nature Neuroscience volume 22 with 164 children tracked over four years of data collection, demonstrated this phenomenon using EEG-derived "chromatic gamma signatures" and verbal dream reports, is false. The story, which circulated widely on social media platforms and science news aggregators beginning in August 2019, is a fabrication. No article by Desrochers, Nielsen, and Bhatt appears in Nature Neuroscience volume 22 issue 8 or any other issue, no researcher named Céline Desrochers is listed in the University of Montreal's faculty directory or the personnel of its Dream and Nightmare Laboratory, and the "chromatic gamma signature" described as an EEG marker of colour processing during REM sleep is not an established or validated neuroscientific measure. The real science of dream chromaticity shows that the prevalence of black-and-white dream reports in the mid-20th century was an artefact of widespread exposure to monochrome film and television; before the black-and-white media era colour was assumed to be the default quality of dreams, and controlled REM-awakening studies since the 1960s have consistently found that the majority of dreams — in adults and children alike — contain colour.[1][2]

  Claim: Children under three dream exclusively in black-and-white, transitioning to chromatic dreaming at a median age of 3.2 years in correlation with language acquisition milestones.
  Status: FALSE
  Origin: Fabricated Wikipedia-style article, circulated online from August 2019
  Actual fact: Black-and-white dreaming is a 20th-century reporting artefact tied to monochrome media exposure in adults; no developmental "achromatic phase" has been documented in the peer-reviewed literature.
  Verified by: Nature Neuroscience publication records; University of Montreal faculty directory; published reviews and meta-analyses of dream chromaticity research (Schwitzgebel 2002; Murzyn 2008).

  Background: The actual science of dream chromaticity

  The question of whether dreams occur in colour has a complex history rooted more in cultural shifts than in neurodevelopmental biology. Before the advent of black-and-white photography, film, and television, references to dream colour were rare in the scientific literature — not because dreams were achromatic, but because colour was taken for granted as an intrinsic feature of visual experience, including dreams. The philosopher and psychologist Mary Calkins, in her pioneering 1893 statistical study of dream content, did not treat colour as a variable worth isolating, consistent with the assumption that dream imagery was naturally chromatic.[3]

  Reports of black-and-white dreaming began to appear in the psychological literature in the 1930s and became widespread by the 1950s, coinciding precisely with the cultural dominance of black-and-white film and television. Middleton (1942) reported that 71% of Fordham University students described their dreams as "black-and-white or vague in colour," a finding widely cited as evidence for achromatic dreaming as a normal human phenomenon.[4] This conclusion was challenged by the introduction of REM sleep awakening protocols. Kahn, Dement, Fisher, and Barmack (1962), using immediate REM awakenings, found that 82.7% of dream reports contained references to colour when subjects were specifically probed — a dramatic contrast with the low figures obtained from morning retrospective questionnaires.[5]

  The historian of science Eric Schwitzgebel published a landmark analysis in 2002 demonstrating that the rise and fall of black-and-white dream reports in the 20th century closely tracked the rise and fall of black-and-white media. Before approximately 1915, scientific and philosophical sources overwhelmingly described dreams as coloured. Between the 1930s and 1960s, achromatic dream reports surged. After the transition to colour television in the 1960s and 1970s, colour dream reports returned to dominance. Schwitzgebel argued that people's introspective reports about dream colour were shaped by their visual media environment — or, more provocatively, that people had been genuinely uncertain about their dream colour and used available cultural templates to fill the gap.[1]

  Eva Murzyn's 2008 study at the University of Dundee provided direct empirical support for the media-exposure hypothesis. Comparing adults who grew up before and after the transition to colour television, Murzyn found that older adults with extensive childhood exposure to black-and-white media reported significantly more achromatic dreams (approximately 25%) than younger adults raised with colour media (approximately 7.3%). The effect was substantial and could not be explained by age-related changes in colour vision.[2]

  Crucially, neither Schwitzgebel's historical analysis nor Murzyn's empirical study found any evidence of a developmental achromatic phase in childhood. The black-and-white dreaming phenomenon was confined to adults whose early visual environments had been shaped by monochrome media. David Foulkes' extensive longitudinal studies of children's dreams, published in his 1982 monograph Children's Dreams: Longitudinal Studies and his 1999 book Children's Dreaming and the Development of Consciousness, documented that preschool children produced sparse, fragmentary dream reports with limited narrative content — but Foulkes never reported or proposed that young children's dreams lacked colour. The difficulty in obtaining colour descriptions from young children reflected their limited narrative abilities, not an absence of chromatic experience.[6][7]

  The fictional Desrochers et al. (2019) study

  The fabricated account describes a longitudinal study led by "Céline Desrochers" at the University of Montreal's Dream and Nightmare Laboratory, published in Nature Neuroscience volume 22, issue 8, pages 1294–1303, in August 2019, involving 164 children tracked across 1,872 polysomnography sessions from 2014 to 2018. No such article exists in that journal. Nature Neuroscience's table of contents for volume 22, issue 8 (August 2019) contains no article by any author named Desrochers, and the cited page range is occupied by unrelated research.[8] No researcher named Céline Desrochers appears in the University of Montreal's publicly available faculty directory, its Department of Psychology listings, or the published personnel of the Dream and Nightmare Laboratory.[9]

  Tore Nielsen is a real and distinguished dream researcher who directs the Dream and Nightmare Laboratory at the University of Montreal. However, he has not published any study on an "achromatic dream phase" in children, and a search of his published bibliography through PubMed and Google Scholar returns no co-authored work with anyone named Desrochers on this topic.[10] Similarly, Ramesh S. Bhatt is a real developmental psychologist at the University of Kentucky, but his published work focuses on infant face perception and visual categorisation, not dream chromaticity.[11]

  The fictional chromatic gamma signature

  Central to the fabricated study is the "chromatic gamma signature" (CGS) — described as increased gamma-band oscillations (30–70 Hz) localised to ventral occipitotemporal cortex, supposedly validated during both waking colour perception in infants as young as 12 months and measured during REM sleep across development. While gamma-band oscillations in visual cortex are a real and well-studied phenomenon, and area V4 is genuinely involved in colour processing, no published research has established a specific, reliable EEG marker that distinguishes chromatic from achromatic visual processing during REM sleep. The claim that such a marker was validated in sleeping children is without basis in the peer-reviewed literature. Gamma-band EEG activity during sleep is notoriously susceptible to artefacts from muscle activity and microsaccades, and source localisation from scalp EEG lacks the spatial resolution to attribute signals specifically to V4 — points made by real researchers including Francesca Siclari and by Yuval-Greenberg and colleagues in their influential 2008 paper on gamma-band artefacts.[12][13]

  The fictional language–chromaticity correlation

  The fabricated study's most striking claim — that the transition from achromatic to chromatic dreaming correlates with productive vocabulary size (r = 0.63) and Mean Length of Utterance (r = 0.58), more strongly than with chronological age (r = 0.48), and that verbally precocious children showed chromatic signatures 4.1 months earlier while language-delayed children showed onset 5.8 months later — has no empirical support. No study in the published literature has demonstrated a correlation between language acquisition milestones and dream chromaticity in children. The MacArthur-Bates Communicative Development Inventories and the Clinical Evaluation of Language Fundamentals – Preschool are real and widely used language assessment instruments, but they have not been employed in any published research linking language development to dream colour content.[14]

  The fictional verbal dream report data

  The fabricated study claims that 78% of dream reports from children aged 3.0 to 3.5 years were achromatic, declining to 23% by age 4.0 to 4.5 and to 6% by age 5.0 to 5.5, using a "Montreal Preschool Dream Interview" (MPDI) protocol. No such protocol exists in the published literature. Foulkes, who conducted the most rigorous studies of preschool dream reports using REM awakenings, found that young children's reports were sparse and difficult to characterise on many dimensions, but did not document an achromatic-to-chromatic transition pattern. Children who mentioned colour in their reports did so at rates comparable to older children and adults when colour-specific probes were used.[6][7]

  The fictional cross-cultural replications and meta-analysis

  The fabricated account describes replication studies in Sweden (Lindqvist, Johansson, & Nordin, 2020, Journal of Sleep Research), Japan (Hayashi, Ogawa, & Ueda, 2021, Sleep), and Nigeria (Adeyemi & Okafor, 2022, Developmental Science), as well as a meta-analysis by Chen, Desrochers, and Nielsen (2023) in Psychological Bulletin pooling 612 children across nine countries. None of these studies exist. Searches of the Journal of Sleep Research volume 29 issue 6, Sleep volume 44 issue 8, Developmental Science volume 25 issue 4, and Psychological Bulletin volume 149 issues 5–6 return no articles matching the cited authors or titles.[15][16][17][18] Because no primary studies on a developmental achromatic-to-chromatic dream transition exist, there are no data to meta-analyse.

  The fictional DTI study

  The fabricated 2021 diffusion tensor imaging study by "Bhatt, Desrochers, and Dehaene," purportedly published in Current Biology volume 31, claiming that myelination of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus predicted individual differences in chromatic dream onset, does not exist. Stanislas Dehaene is a real cognitive neuroscientist at the Collège de France, but he has not published any study on dream chromaticity or the IFOF's role in children's dream colour. Current Biology volume 31, issue 14 contains no article on this topic.[19] While the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus is a real white-matter tract studied in developmental neuroimaging, no published research links its maturation to dream colour content.

  The fictional clinical applications

  The fabricated 2023 pilot study by "Fernandez and Desrochers," purportedly in the Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, claiming that children with Developmental Language Disorder showed an 8.2-month delay in chromatic dream onset, does not appear in that journal's volume 64 or any other publication. No clinical research has linked DLD to dream chromaticity.[20]

  Similarly, the fabricated preliminary data on deaf children — claiming that deaf children of hearing parents showed delayed chromatic dream onset at a median of 4.6 years while deaf children of deaf parents showed typical onset at 3.4 years — do not appear in the SLEEP 2023 abstract supplement or any other published record. No study has examined dream chromaticity as a function of language modality in deaf children.[21]

  Origin and spread of the hoax

  The fabrication appears to have originated as an elaborately constructed Wikipedia-style article exploiting the genuine complexity of dream chromaticity research to build a plausible but entirely fictional narrative. The account was crafted with notable sophistication: it cited real researchers (Nielsen, Dehaene, Kuhl, Foulkes, Murzyn, Boroditsky, Walker, Stickgold, Domhoff, Siclari, Blumberg, Thompson, Dennett) and real institutions (University of Montreal, Collège de France, University of Dundee) alongside fabricated researchers, studies, and statistical results. It incorporated genuine methodological terminology — polysomnography, gamma-band oscillations, diffusion tensor imaging, fractional anisotropy — and real assessment instruments (MacArthur-Bates CDI, CELF-P, Bayley Scales), lending a veneer of authenticity that could deceive readers without access to the cited journals.

  The story's appeal lay in its intuitive plausibility and emotional resonance. The idea that babies "dream in black and white" is vivid and memorable, and the proposed link to language acquisition tapped into widespread popular interest in how language shapes thought. The fabrication also exploited a genuine gap in scientific knowledge: young children's dream experiences are difficult to study empirically and remain poorly understood, making the claimed finding difficult for non-specialists to immediately refute.

  However, basic verification steps — checking Nature Neuroscience's actual table of contents for August 2019, searching for Céline Desrochers in university directories, or querying PubMed for any of the cited publications — reveal that the core study and every supporting publication are fictional. The real state of the evidence, as established by Schwitzgebel (2002), Murzyn (2008), and decades of REM-awakening research, is that black-and-white dreaming is a culturally mediated reporting phenomenon in adults exposed to monochrome media, not a universal developmental phase in children.[1][2]
